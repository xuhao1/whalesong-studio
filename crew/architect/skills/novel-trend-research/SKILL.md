# Research for a story

A find from the web is raw material, not a plot point. It earns its place only when a reader of this book would feel it belongs there.

## Look with a question

Search for what the plan needs, not for "ideas". Good questions name the gap:

- **What is alive right now around this theme?** The topic the chapter touches, plus the year or "最近" / "this month"; what people argue about; which phrase everyone is repeating. Look at two or three sources — one post is an anecdote, three are a current.
- **How did this really work?** A trade, a tool, a ritual, a law, a route, a price. One concrete procedure beats a page of atmosphere.
- **What would a reader of this genre expect, and be tired of?** The trope lists and reader complaints of the genre are a map of what to turn.

Search in the language of the book's readers first. Open the page (`web_fetch`) before you rely on a search result's snippet: snippets are often wrong about what the page says.

## Keep little

For each thing you keep, write down: what it is in one sentence, where it came from (the URL), and when it is from. Drop anything you cannot date, anything only one source says, and anything that is someone's own creative work — another novel's twist, a comic's joke told in full, a song's words. A meme's *shape* (the format, the turn of phrase everyone varies) is common property; a creator's specific text is not yours to lift.

## Translate before you use

Ask of every find: **what is this in my book's world?**

- A contemporary meme in a period or secondary-world story cannot appear as itself. Take what makes it work — the reversal, the deadpan, the escalation — and rebuild it from things the world has. A reader who knows the meme smiles; a reader who does not notices nothing wrong.
- In a present-day story a meme can appear as itself, but it dates the page. Put it in a character's mouth, where dating is characterization, not in the narration.
- A real procedure belongs to a character who would know it. Let it cost them something or get them something in the scene; a fact that only decorates is the first thing an editor cuts.
- A trend the genre's readers are tired of is a setup: play its opening straight and turn it.

Check each against the book before you propose it (`book_search`, `book_read`): does it contradict a rule of the world, a character's knowledge, something already on the page?

## Report

Give the showrunner proposals, not a reading list. For each: the beat or detail as it would appear in this book; which chapter or thread it serves; what it rests on (source and date); what it costs (a setup needed earlier, a rule it leans on). Say plainly which finds you looked at and set aside, in a line each — it saves the next search.
